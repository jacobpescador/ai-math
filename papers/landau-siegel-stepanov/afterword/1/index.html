The question

Euler noticed that n2+n+41 is prime for every n from 0 to 39. The reason is that in ℚ(−163) every prime below 41 is inert: it stays prime instead of splitting into two conjugate factors. Since 4(n2+n+41)=(2n+1)2+163, no odd inert prime can divide a value, and the values are odd, so they have no prime factor below 41, and for n≤39 they are below 412.

In every quadratic field, half of the primes split and half stay inert in the long run, so the question is how long that balance can take to set in. Write χ(p)=−1 when p is inert and χ(p)=+1 when it splits. Each inert prime contributes a factor (1+1/p)−1<1 to the Euler product for L(1,χ), so a long run of inert primes heuristically makes L(1,χ) small, and for an imaginary field the class number with it. A real zero of L(s,χ) very close to 1, a Landau–Siegel zero, would heuristically make most primes inert over a range much longer than the size q of the discriminant, which is Euler’s run on a far larger scale. Until October 2026 nobody could rule one out. OpenAI’s argument that there are none is dated 1 October, and shorter ones followed. Our paper proves a bound of Barreto’s: with S−(X) the sum of (logp)/p over the inert primes p≤X,

S−(X)≤12logX+14logq+loglogX+O(1)

for all X≥3, uniformly in q. The sum over all primes is about logX, so the inert primes never take much more than their fair half, plus a quarter of logq.

The idea in one variable

The common strategy is to build a nonzero determinant, bound its size from above, and bound it from below by the prime powers that divide it. A determinant suits both bounds. Hadamard’s inequality controls its size through the lengths of its rows, and adding multiples of other rows to a row leaves it unchanged, so a congruence between rows becomes a factor of the determinant. The simplest case proves Mertens’ bound ∑p≤N(logp)/p≤logN+O(1). Let V be the Vandermonde determinant of the monomials zj, 0≤j<N, at the points 0,…,N−1. For a prime p, replace the row of zj, with j=ph+v and 0≤v<p, by the row of zv(zp−z)h. That only adds earlier rows, and by Fermat’s little theorem the new row is divisible by ph. Hence V is divisible by about pN2/2p, while Hadamard’s inequality gives log|V|≤12N2logN. Each unit of degree buys (logp)/p of divisibility and costs logN of size, and comparing the two is Mertens’ bound. Here V=1!2!⋯(N−1)! is plainly not zero.

The same idea in two variables

In a quadratic field ℚ(d), with d the fundamental discriminant and |d|=q, take points (α,α′), with α=x+yd for integers x,y in a box and α′=x−yd. At an odd inert prime p, raising to the pth power is conjugation modulo p, so these points lie on the curve w=zp modulo p, and zp−w plays the part of zp−z. At a split prime the pth power map is not conjugation, so the argument sees only the inert primes, the sum a Landau–Siegel zero would make large.

On the curve w=zp the monomials ziwj and zi−pwj+1 take the same values: modulo p they collide. The rewriting zv(zp−w)hwj, which goes back to OpenAI’s proof, turns each collision into a factor of p, as in one variable. But it brings in powers of w, which add size and no divisibility, so the rows are two-variable monomials cut off by a weight, i+ℓj<p0 for a large inert prime p0 and ℓ about logp0, about p02/2ℓ of them, far more than there are small points. Using more points makes them larger, and the bound becomes worse than trivial. Green’s answer is to take z-derivatives at the same points: a value and its first t−1 derivatives at one point impose t conditions, as t points would, without enlarging any point, and they keep the divisibility because the derivative of zp is pzp−1.

Where does the quarter of logq come from? Keeping both conjugates of α=x+yd within size H forces |x|≤H and |y|≤H/q, so for H≥q there are only about H2/q such points. To have p0 of them, H must be about p0q1/4, which needs q≤p02, and Barreto’s box, about q times longer in x than in y, achieves this (paper, §1). Comparing size and divisibility then gives, heuristically (made precise in §3),

∑ℓ<p<p0p inertlogpp≲logH≈12logp0+14logq.

The 12 reflects that the points fill a plane, and the 14 that the plane is stretched by q in one direction.

Why the number is not zero

The divisibility came from monomials colliding modulo small primes. Nonvanishing will come from a prime at which they cannot collide. To count roots along a curve w=g(z) through all the points, differentiating in z has to commute with substituting w=g(z), and by the chain rule that forces g′=0. Over ℂ this makes g constant, and a horizontal line holds at most one point. Our first proof, with a Wronskian over ℂ, worked around this at the cost of about twice as many derivatives. But modulo a prime p0, the polynomial zp0 has derivative zero, and if p0 is inert its graph passes through every point.

So take p0 to be the largest inert prime up to X, which loses nothing because no inert prime lies between p0 and X, and keep all z-degrees below p0. Substituting w=zp0 sends ziwj to zi+p0j, a two-digit number in base p0, so distinct monomials stay distinct, and the derivatives pass through the substitution. The box holds p0 points that stay distinct modulo p0, and a relation among the rows would make the one-variable polynomial vanish at each of them to order one more than its largest w-degree, giving more roots, counted with multiplicity, than its degree (paper, Lemma 4). This is the root count at the heart of Stepanov’s method for counting points on curves over finite fields, used the other way round: there it bounds the number of points, and here it shows that no relation among the rows exists.

One substitution therefore does both jobs. At the small inert primes the exponents collide, and the collisions are the divisibility. At p0 they cannot, and that is the nonvanishing (Figure 1).

What it does not do

The absolute constant in the O(1) has not been made explicit. With it, the bound would give a numerical limit on a tenth imaginary quadratic field of class number one, whose primes below (q+1)/4 are all inert, so that S−(q/4) is about logq against the 34logq allowed. For X≤q the bound says less than Mertens’ theorem, the 12 cannot be lowered, and we know of no argument that lowers the 14. The proofs are in §§2–4 of the paper, and its §5 compares the other arguments.
