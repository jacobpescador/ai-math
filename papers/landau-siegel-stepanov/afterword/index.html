AI Math · Landau–Siegel zeros and a Stepanov-type nonvanishing lemma

Afterword

Edition 2, 22 October 2026 · on v3 of the paper, 22 October 2026 · how this was written

Forty primes

Euler’s polynomial n2+n+41 is prime for every n from 0 to 39. Nothing about it looks special until it is multiplied by four:

4(n2+n+41)=(2n+1)2+163.

So if an odd prime p≠163 divides a value, −163 is a square modulo p, and p splits in ℚ(−163). The values are odd, and for n≤39 they stay below 412. A composite value would need a prime factor below 41, and that prime would split. None does. In ℚ(−163) every prime below 41 is inert, and the forty primes are that single fact read off forty times.

A field cannot keep this up for ever. In every quadratic field half the primes split and half stay inert, in the long run, and the books are kept by L(1,χ). Each inert prime puts a factor (1+1/p)−1<1 into its Euler product, and for an imaginary field of discriminant −q, q>4, the class number is qL(1,χ)/π. At q=163 that makes L(1,χ)=π/163, about a quarter: a long run of inert primes is a small value at 1.

A Landau–Siegel zero is a real zero β of L(s,χ) with (1−β)logq small. Heuristically it would make most primes inert over a range much longer than q, Euler’s run stretched far past the size of the discriminant. Siegel’s bound L(1,χ)≫εq−ε comes with a constant no one can compute. Excluding the zero gives the effective L(1,χ)≫1/logq, and for odd χ a class number ≫q/logq (paper, §1).

OpenAI’s argument that there are no such zeros is dated 1 October 2026. Shorter ones followed within weeks: Kerr’s, Green’s, ours, and then Barreto’s and van Dijk’s. The current version of our paper proves Barreto’s bound

S−(X)≤12logX+14logq+loglogX+O(1),

uniformly in X≥3 and q, where S−(X) sums (logp)/p over the primes p≤X with χ(p)=−1. If every one of those primes were inert the sum would be logX+O(1), by Mertens. Its leading terms allow less than that as soon as X passes q, and little more than half of it once X is a large power of q.

The idea of the proof is in the next two sections and in “Stepanov” and “The half and the quarter”. The rest is how we got there and where the method stops.

A determinant that knows Mertens

The whole mechanism fits in one variable. The model is Tao’s, from a comment on his blog.

Take the Vandermonde determinant V of the monomials zj, 0≤j<N, at the integers 0,1,…,N−1. It equals 1!2!⋯(N−1)!. Now fix a prime p, write j=ph+v with 0≤v<p, and replace the row of zj by the row of zv(zp−z)h. That polynomial is zj plus lower powers, so V does not change. By Fermat, ap−a is divisible by p for every integer a, so the new row is divisible by ph, and summing h=⌊j/p⌋ over the rows shows that pN2/2p, roughly, divides V.

Hadamard’s inequality bounds the other side: row j has entries at most Nj, so log|V|≤12N2logN, roughly. Put every prime up to N into the lower bound and compare:

N22∑p≤Nlogpp≲N22logN,

which is Mertens’ bound ∑p≤N(logp)/p≤logN+O(1), recovered from a determinant.

Two things in this toy come free. Fermat holds at every prime, so the comparison bounds the sum over all of them and can say nothing about a character. And the determinant is visibly nonzero. A proof about χ has to give up the first and then pay for the second.

Two variables

To see χ the points must be quadratic. Write χ as the Kronecker symbol (d/·) with |d|=q, let α=x+yd range over a box, and use the pairs (α,α′) as points (z,w). At an odd inert prime p the ring ℤ[d]/p is the field of p2 elements and conjugation is its Frobenius, so αp≡α′(modp) (paper, Lemma 3). Modulo p every point lies on the curve w=zp.

On that curve ziwj and zi−pwj+1 take the same values. The monomials collide, and the rewriting

ziwj ⟼ zv(zp−w)hwj,i=ph+v,

which comes from OpenAI’s proof in Kerr’s form, leaves the determinant unchanged and turns each collision into a factor p. It is the Vandermonde trick with zp−w where zp−z was, and it works at the inert primes only.

Two variables bring a cost the toy did not have. A power of w makes the entries larger and adds nothing to the divisibility, which is counted in z-degree. So the rows are weighted, ziwj with i+ℓj<p0 and ℓ about logp0, about p02/2ℓ of them. A square determinant wants as many columns. With values alone that means about p02/2ℓ points, and a set of points that large is tall enough to make the bound worse than Mertens.

Green’s answer was to take z-derivatives at the same points. A value and t−1 derivatives give t conditions per point, and the divisibility survives, because the derivative of zp is pzp−1.

What the derivatives keep out of the count

The derivatives were brought in to save points. Working out why the proof still saw χ, we found they do a second job.

At an odd split prime p, ℤ[d]/p is 𝔽p×𝔽p and conjugation swaps the factors. Frobenius is the identity there, so at every point zp≡z and wp≡w. With values alone, zp−z certifies divisibility by split primes exactly as zp−w certifies it by inert ones, the comparison counts every prime, and a bound on all primes is only Mertens. Values alone are the one-variable toy wearing a second coordinate.

With derivatives the two congruences part company, on one line:

∂z(zp−w)≡0,∂z(zp−z)≡−1(modp).

Differentiating a factor zp−w leaves something still divisible by p, while differentiating a factor zp−z leaves a unit. Each order of derivative can therefore strip one factor zp−z from a row, and above ℓ a row has no more such factors than there are orders, so counting them guarantees nothing. The other relation, wp≡w, survives differentiation, but it acts through the w-degree, which the weight holds to a 1/ℓ share, and it adds at most about (logp0)/ℓ. The derivatives that save the 12 also keep the split primes above ℓ out of the count, and what the argument bounds is S−, the sum a Landau–Siegel zero makes large.

We tested this in ℤ[−2], where 3 splits, with the eight monomials 1,z,…,z5,w,zw. The first example we computed took values and first derivatives at 0,1,2,−2, four points distinct modulo 3. The determinant was 80(47−6−2), of norm 28·52·2281, with no factor 3, and we took that as the pattern: derivatives kill the split primes.

Three hundred random point sets, distinct modulo 3, corrected us. With values alone at eight points the norm was divisible by 3 every time. With values and first derivatives at four points it was prime to 3 in only about a third of the cases (appendix), and our first example had landed in that lucky third. Derivatives do not forbid divisibility by a split prime. They stop guaranteeing it, and a bound can use only what is guaranteed. In these examples the w-degree stops at 1, so w3≡w never had room to act.

A Wronskian, and a determinant that vanished

Our first, unpublished argument used Green’s points and derivatives, weighted rows with a weight growing with X, a square box of points, and a Wronskian over ℂ to show the determinant is nonzero. It proved a bound with 12logq, and it was wasteful. In the Wronskian each derivative, taken up to about the largest w-degree s≈p0/ℓ, lowers the order of every zero by one, so a zero of order t survives only as order about t−s, and about 2s orders of derivative were needed. In small exact cases it asked for up to four times the conditions that full rank actually required.

The waste cost only a constant in the final bound. What bothered us was what it implied: a zero estimate that loose is probably the wrong zero estimate.

The tempting repair is to assume the determinant cannot vanish. It can. Take the twelve monomials ziwj with i+2j≤5, and values with first and second z-derivatives at the four points (a+bu,a−bu), a,b∈{0,1}. The determinant is

576u6(u2−1)(u2+7)(7u2+1),

which vanishes at u=−7, where the four points have distinct coordinates. They are the conjugate pairs of the box {x+y−7:x,y∈{0,1}}, a small box of the kind the proof uses. Random integer points gave full rank in every test we ran, which is no comfort when the points come from a structured box.

Counting roots along a curve is the natural way to prove nonvanishing, and over ℂ that route is closed. To turn F(z,w) into one variable along w=g(z) and keep track of multiplicities, ∂z has to commute with the substitution, which forces g′=0. Then g is constant, and a line w=c holds at most one of the points.

Stepanov

Stepanov’s method, for counting points on curves over finite fields, works with polynomials in x and xp and differentiates them, and the derivative does not see xp. That is the missing commutation. Modulo p0, zp0 has derivative zero, so along w=zp0 differentiating in z commutes with substituting for w. If p0 is inert, that graph of Frobenius passes through every one of our points. The curve that cannot exist over ℂ exists modulo p0, and it is the curve the divisibility was already using.

Take p0 to be the largest inert prime up to X, which loses nothing since S−(X)=S−(p0), and keep every z-degree below p0. Under w=zp0 the monomial ziwj becomes zi+p0j, and the exponent is a two-digit number in base p0, so distinct monomials stay distinct. A relation among the rows modulo p0 becomes a nonzero polynomial G(z)=F(z,zp0) of degree below p0(s+1) with roots of multiplicity s+1 at p0 or more distinct residues. That is too many roots (paper, Lemma 4). Stepanov builds such a polynomial to bound the number of points on a curve. Here the points are given, and the count shows the polynomial is zero. It needs s+1 orders of derivative, about half of what the Wronskian needed.

This root count is ours: it has been the paper’s nonvanishing argument since its first version. Van Dijk’s note uses it too.

Looking back, one substitution does both jobs. At a small inert prime p the z-degrees run past p, monomials collide on w=zp, and the collisions are the divisibility. At p0 the z-degrees are too small for any collision, every monomial keeps its own exponent, and that is the nonvanishing.

The half and the quarter

Call max(|α|,|α′|) the height of a point. Both conjugates within H forces |x|≤H and |y|≤H/q, so there are about H2/q points of height at most H, once H≥q. The root count needs p0 points, so H is about p0q1/4, which is at least q exactly when q≤p02. Barreto’s rectangular box, long in x and short in y, achieves it. Hadamard against divisibility then gives, heuristically, S−≲logH≈12logp0+14logq (paper, §1).

The 12 is there because the derivatives cut the points to about p0. The count asks for p0(s+1)≈p02/ℓ conditions against about p02/2ℓ rows, twice over-determined. On the rectangle of exponents i<p0, j≤s it would be exactly square, but the rewriting needs the triangle (paper, after Lemma 4). With values alone the coefficient of logX would be 1, worse than Mertens. The 12 cannot be lowered: for fixed q, S−(X)=12logX+Oq(1).

The 14 is the stretch by q, under a square root. A square box of p0 points has height about p0q and gives 12logq, which is what our version 1 proved.

The rectangle came late because the proofs with a fixed weight, OpenAI’s and Kerr’s, worked at X a large power of q, where the term in logq is small beside logX. It shows most plainly in bounds uniform in X, like our version 1’s, where p0 can be as small as q. When q≤p02 the rectangle is optimal for this size estimate. The present version of the paper takes Barreto’s box, and so proves his bound.

Why the constant is 1

The corollary is (1−β)logq≥1−O(loglogq/logq). Put T(x)=∑p≤xχ(p)(logp)/p. By Mertens, T(x)≈logx−2S−(x), and the 12logX in the theorem cancels the logx exactly:

T(x)≥−2·14logq−2loglogx−O(loglogq).

The quarter is counted twice. Averaging T over logarithmic scales gives −L′/L just to the right of 1, the Hadamard product bounds it the other way with 12logq from the conductor, and together they give 1/(u+1−β)≤logq up to small terms, so (1−β)logq≥1−o(1). The 1 is 1/(12+2·14) (paper, §4 and Remark 7).

The exact cancellation is what forces the growing weight. A fixed ℓ gives a coefficient 12+ε in front of logX, and that leaves −2εlogx in T, which the averaging does not absorb, and the constant drops below 1. So ℓ has to grow with X. The price is loglogX: the rewriting trades p units of z-degree for one power of w, which costs ℓ, so the rewritten rows stay inside the weighted triangle only for p≥ℓ. The inert primes below ℓ are lost, and they are worth up to logℓ.

Two pictures that needed correcting

The lemma invites explanations, and two that came easily needed correcting.

The first was that modulo p0 all the points lie on one integral curve of ∂z. That is true and says nothing. In characteristic p every curve w=g(zp) is an integral curve of ∂z, and any finite set of points with distinct z-coordinates lies on one. What the count uses is that the curve is w=zp0, of degree exactly p0. Nor is the auxiliary prime the reason the determinant is nonzero. It is one way of proving it. Barreto proves it with a Wronskian and a minor in ℤ, and the auxiliary prime does it more briefly.

The second was to read the root count as Bézout’s theorem for F=0 against w=zp0. The polynomial F does not have low total degree, and plane Bézout allows about p02 intersections, far more than the count can tolerate. Bézout with weight p0 on w gives exactly the root count. The two-digit numbers in base p0 were the weighted degree all along.

Where it stops

The 12logX is sharp. The 14logq is the best this size estimate gives, and nobody has lowered it. In small cases (p0≤43) the logarithm of the determinant’s norm was 80 to 90 percent of Hadamard’s bound for it, which leaves little room there and says nothing about large sizes. Our guess, and it is only a guess, is that going below 14 needs divisibility by split primes, which the derivatives keep out of the count on purpose.

The loglogX we can now explain as forced. Any set of rows closed under the rewriting at p satisfies I≤pJ+(p−1)m, where I and J total the z- and w-degrees and m counts the rows (paper, Remark 6). Such a set carries about a 1/p share of w-degree, or loses as much in the divisibility count. By the Chinese remainder theorem there are fields with every prime up to clogX inert, and in them an argument built this way pays loglogX whatever the weight. Removing it would take sharper estimates or a new mechanism, and would not improve the corollary, since the primes dividing q cost loglogq anyway.

Below X=q the bound says less than Mertens, and the reason is Fermat. When q>p02 the p0 smallest points are essentially the integers 0,…,p0−1, where α′=α and αp≡α′ is Fermat’s little theorem at every prime. The construction cannot tell inert from split there. It is back in one variable, and it can only reproduce Mertens.

The error terms do not decay, so the inequality sees a real zero only within about 1/logq of 1. The weight (logp)/p is not what limits this. By partial summation, a two-sided estimate ∑n≤xχ(n)Λ(n)/n=C+O(x−δ) would give a power saving and a zero-free half-plane Res>1−δ. OpenAI’s quasi-Riemann-hypothesis preprints claim zero-free regions of fixed width by automorphic methods. Whether a polynomial argument could reach that is open.

Pratt remarked that OpenAI’s, Kerr’s and Green’s arguments recall Dobrowolski’s bound for Lehmer’s problem. There the comparison runs the other way round: one algebraic number, a polynomial vanishing to high order at it, and a lower bound for its height. The parallel is structural, with no character in it. As far as we know nobody has carried the auxiliary prime or the rectangle across, and whether either survives the crossing is a question we would like answered.

Class number one

The O(1) in our theorem is not explicit. As Silberman pointed out, with it the bound would limit a tenth field of class number one. In such a field, which would have q>163, every prime below (q+1)/4 is inert, since a split prime p would be the norm (a2+qb2)/4 of an integer of the field with b≠0, which is at least (q+1)/4. For q=163 the cut is 41. So S−(q/4)≈logq, while the bound allows about 34logq. With the square box’s 12logq the two tie, and the field is ruled out through the corollary instead. With the 14 the inequality forbids Euler’s run directly, once the field is large enough.

Van Dijk’s version 2 makes his argument explicit: class number one implies a discriminant below 8·109, and a finite sieve leaves the nine known fields. The largest of them is ℚ(−163), where every prime below 41 is inert and n2+n+41 is prime for forty values of n.

Appendix: the computations

These are exact computations in small cases. They check individual steps and say nothing about the asymptotics.

The split-prime filter. In ℤ[−2] the monomials are 1,z,…,z5,w,zw. In 300 random choices of points distinct modulo 3, values alone at eight points gave norms divisible by 36 to 317, while values and first derivatives at four points gave norms prime to 3 in 104 cases and divisible by 32 to 39 in the rest. The first pair of examples we computed was this. Values alone at 0,1,2,3 and k+−2 for k=−1,0,1,2, eight points that are not all distinct modulo 3, gave the determinant 210·33·5·(1−−2), up to sign. Values and first derivatives at 0,1,2,−2, which are distinct modulo 3, gave 80(47−6−2), whose norm is 28·52·2281. The factor 3 is absent, as it is in about a third of the cases.

The singular square system. The monomials are ziwj with i+2j≤5, and the conditions are values and first and second z-derivatives at the four points (a+bu,a−bu) with a,b∈{0,1}. The determinant is 576u6(u2−1)(u2+7)(7u2+1).

Random points. For three small sizes of the root count’s square system, twenty random choices of integer points each gave full rank in characteristic 0. Points on a line w=c always gave a singular system.

Other tests. At 84 small parameter choices, exact ranks showed full rank as soon as the conditions were as many as the monomials, while the Wronskian asked for up to four times as many. For p0≤43 the logarithm of the determinant’s norm was 80 to 90 percent of Hadamard’s bound for it. The split-prime divisibility without derivatives was checked in smaller cases.
